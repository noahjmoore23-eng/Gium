How I sell

Most sales sites list adjectives. This is the sequence behind the numbers above, so a sales leader can tell in one read whether it matches their motion.

01

Read the business first

Before the first call I want to know how the account makes money, where the margin leaks, and which number their boss asks about on Monday. Discovery should confirm a hypothesis, not start from zero.

02

Qualify honestly

Pain, budget, decision process, and a real reason to act this quarter. If one of the four is missing I say so out loud and either fix it or move on. A clean no beats a slow maybe, and it keeps the forecast worth reading.

03

Sell to the P&L

A finance background means I can put the proposal in the buyer’s own terms — cost per unit, payback period, what it does to next year’s budget. One screen that moves a number they already track beats ten that are merely impressive.

04

Earn the second sale

Three years in client operations taught me what an oversold deal costs the people who have to deliver it. I close on a plan with a named signer and dated steps, and I don’t promise things implementation will have to walk back.

Case study

Gium — I built working software for an industry to learn how it runs

Moving companies lose money in two predictable places: quotes that miss and crews that idle. An estimator eyeballs a three-bedroom house and guesses low, so two movers get sent on a job that needed four, the crew runs into overtime, and the hourly minimum never covers it. Meanwhile the dispatcher works off a whiteboard and double-books a truck.

Gium is my answer to both. It’s a working web app: a room-by-room cube sheet of 82 standard carrier items that converts an inventory into packed volume, shipment weight, recommended crew size, the smallest truck that fits, and a phase-by-phase hour breakdown. It prices off a single rate card — hourly against a minimum under 100 miles, weight-based line haul plus mileage and fuel past that, the way an interstate move is actually tariffed. Quoted jobs land on a week board that flags a mover on two overlapping jobs, a double-booked truck, or a job understaffed against its own estimate.

Why it’s on a sales site: nobody asked me to build it. It’s the same instinct behind the quota numbers — go far enough into how a business runs that you can talk to an operations manager about cube sheets, access penalties, tariffs, and crew utilization without reaching for a script. That’s the preparation I’d bring to a territory.
